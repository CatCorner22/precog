# Precog commercial validation and research plan

**Prepared:** 2026-09-22  
**Scope:** small and midsize dental groups, their financial/compliance advisors,
and adjacent small-business control workflows  
**Decision horizon:** 12 weeks for initial go/no-go evidence; 12 months for a
commercially credible system of record

## 1. Honest starting position

Precog is a strong **control-design and advisory prototype**. It is not yet a
commercial control-operations platform.

The current product can explain responsibilities, visualize authority,
identify modeled conflicts, stress-test continuity, compare assessment
snapshots, and assemble an evidence-backed value narrative. Those capabilities
are differentiated and demo well. They do not yet prove that a customer's real
system access matches the model or that a selected control operated throughout
the period.

Commercial viability is plausible, not proven. The best initial thesis is:

> Precog helps dental advisors and growing dental groups establish who can do
> what, reconcile approved responsibility to actual access, collect evidence,
> and close control gaps without buying enterprise GRC software.

The weakest thesis is a low-price, self-service fraud-prevention dashboard for
solo practices. Very small practices have few people available to segregate,
limited implementation capacity, and a high support-to-subscription ratio.

### The decisive commercial question

Will a named buyer pay for and repeatedly complete a quarterly workflow that
keeps people, access, evidence, exceptions, and remediation synchronized?

If the answer is no, additional scoring sophistication will not create a viable
business. If the answer is yes, Precog has a credible vertical wedge.

## 2. Commercial hypotheses to test

Every hypothesis must have an owner, evidence source, deadline, and explicit
pass/fail threshold.

| ID | Hypothesis | Test | Pass threshold | Failure implication |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| H1 | Dental advisors experience repeatable responsibility/access review pain | 12 advisor interviews + workflow observation | 8 describe the same recurring job; 5 provide artifacts | Reframe the buyer or stop advisor-first development |
| H2 | 3–50-location groups will fund the workflow | 10 buyer interviews + 5 priced proposals | 3 paid design partners | Treat as consulting aid, not standalone SaaS |
| H3 | A credible first model can be created quickly | Timed onboarding with real staff/access exports | Median under 4 hours advisor-led; under 1 day total | Reduce ontology and automate ingestion before adding analysis |
| H4 | Findings are accurate enough to act on | Blind finding review by customer + independent expert | At least 70% valid; at least 50% actionable | Tune rules and suppress heuristics before scaling sales |
| H5 | Customers will maintain the model | 90-day pilot with two review cycles | 60% complete cycle two; 85% assigned certifications complete | Product lacks recurring ownership; do not claim continuous controls |
| H6 | Value can be demonstrated without speculative fraud savings | Baseline/time study + exception outcomes | Each paid pilot shows labor savings, recovery, or approved risk closure worth annual price | Narrow use case or lower implementation burden |
| H7 | Advisor distribution lowers acquisition cost | Multi-client advisor pilot | One advisor activates 3+ paying clients | Sell direct or find a different channel |
| H8 | CSV reconciliation is sufficient for the first sale | Import real exports from PMS/accounting/payroll/bank | 80% of permissions map; review can resolve the remainder | Build one connector only after identifying the blocking source |

## 3. Target segments and buyer map

### Priority 1 — dental CPAs, fractional CFOs, and compliance advisors

**Economic buyer:** partner, practice leader, or advisory-services director.  
**Daily user:** manager, senior associate, or fractional controller.  
**Trigger:** annual controls review, acquisition diligence, suspected loss,
insurance renewal, lender request, or client growth.  
**Job to be done:** deliver a consistent review across many clients with less
spreadsheet work and a defensible audit trail.  
**What they must believe:** Precog improves realization, standardizes quality,
and produces a client-ready result without replacing professional judgment.

### Priority 2 — dental groups with 3–50 locations

**Economic buyer:** CFO, COO, controller, owner, or compliance leader.  
**Daily user:** finance manager, IT/access administrator, regional operator, or
internal auditor.  
**Trigger:** acquisition, employee turnover, payment loss, centralization,
access cleanup, insurance renewal, or external audit.  
**Job to be done:** know who can initiate, approve, record, hold, and reconcile
high-risk activity across locations and systems.

### Priority 3 — acquisition and integration teams

Sell a fixed-scope assessment before attempting a broad subscription. The
deliverable should include the responsibility map, actual-access inventory,
critical conflicts, continuity gaps, accepted compensating controls, and a
time-bound remediation program.

### Defer

- Solo practices, except through an advisor bundle.
- Enterprise DSOs requiring SSO/SCIM, extensive connectors, procurement,
  formal SLAs, and mature assurance before purchase.
- Insurer or lender distribution until customer outcome evidence exists.
- Generic small-business positioning until the dental workflow is repeatable.

## 4. Subject-matter research program

### 4.1 Internal-control model

Build an evidence table for every entitlement, conflict rule, control measure,
and fallback. Each record should contain:

- authoritative principle or source;
- dental workflow interpretation;
- risk or failure mode;
- directive, preventive, detective, and corrective options;
- optimal segregation and acceptable small-team fallback;
- evidence expected;
- testing procedure, sample, frequency, and reviewer qualification;
- known limitations and circumstances requiring expert advice;
- ontology version, effective date, editor, and approver.

Research anchors to validate directly include COSO Internal Control, the 2025
GAO Green Book, NIST CSF 2.0, HHS HIPAA Security Rule risk-analysis guidance,
FBI business-email-compromise guidance, applicable state privacy/breach rules,
bank treasury-management practices, payroll-provider controls, and dental
professional guidance. Legal or regulatory interpretations require qualified
counsel; the product should distinguish requirements from leading practices.

### 4.2 Field validation panels

Recruit a standing editorial panel:

- 2 dental CPAs or outsourced controllers;
- 2 dental operations leaders;
- 1 dental biller/RCM specialist;
- 1 fraud examiner;
- 1 healthcare privacy/security specialist;
- 1 managed IT or identity administrator;
- 1 insurance or banking risk specialist;
- 1 owner from a small practice who must use compensating controls.

Run rule review in blinded rounds. Ask each expert to rate correctness,
severity, applicability, false-positive risk, recommended evidence, and viable
fallback. Publish disagreements and confidence rather than forcing false
consensus.

### 4.3 Workflow observation

Do not rely only on interviews. Observe at least eight real workflows:

1. daily collections, closeout, and deposit;
2. payment and ERA posting;
3. refunds, adjustments, credits, and write-offs;
4. vendor setup, invoice entry, ACH/check release;
5. payroll changes, processing, and release;
6. joiner/mover/leaver access changes;
7. month-end bank and ledger reconciliation;
8. backup monitoring and recovery testing.

Capture systems touched, actors, handoffs, evidence, exception paths, elapsed
time, rework, informal workarounds, and where the documented process differs
from reality. Do not collect patient data unless necessary and contractually
permitted.

## 5. Competitive research plan

The comparison must be reproducible and dated. Use official product pages,
demos, help centers, security documentation, contracts, and customer references;
do not treat vendor marketing language as verified capability.

### Categories to benchmark

1. **Dental analytics:** Dental Intelligence, Practice by Numbers, Jarvis
   Analytics, and analytics embedded in major PMS platforms.
2. **Dental platforms and RCM:** Planet DDS/Denticon, CareStack, and relevant
   claims, payment, and eligibility vendors.
3. **Clinical AI:** Overjet and Pearl—not direct substitutes, but competitors for
   executive attention and budget with clearer transaction-level ROI.
4. **Enterprise SoD/identity/GRC:** Pathlock, SafePaaS, Fastpath, SAP/Oracle risk
   products, AuditBoard, and Workiva.
5. **Substitutes:** spreadsheets, CPA workpapers, insurer questionnaires,
   managed IT reviews, access reports, and owner review.

### Benchmark dimensions

Score only observed capability and attach evidence:

- target customer and buyer;
- implementation time and required services;
- pricing unit and minimum contract;
- PMS/accounting/payroll/bank/identity connectors;
- actual-access ingestion and unmatched-permission handling;
- SoD policy depth and configurability;
- small-team compensating-control support;
- evidence requests, testing, conclusions, and remediation;
- certification campaigns and reminders;
- immutable audit history;
- multi-location and advisor/client tenancy;
- reports, exports, API, and data portability;
- SSO/SCIM, tenant isolation, encryption, recovery, SOC 2, and BAA posture;
- accessibility and mobile/narrow-screen usability;
- observed-value reporting and customer proof;
- contract lock-in and implementation burden.

### Mystery-shop protocol

Use the same scenario for every vendor: a five-location group with 85 workers,
one PMS, cloud accounting, outsourced payroll, two bank platforms, recent
acquisitions, and quarterly access review. Ask each vendor to demonstrate
vendor setup/payment conflicts, refund/adjustment conflicts, terminated-user
access, evidence retention, reviewer sign-off, and cross-location reporting.

## 6. Customer discovery instruments

### Buyer interview — 45 minutes

1. Tell us about the last time you reviewed responsibilities or access.
2. What triggered it, who owned it, and what was delivered?
3. Show the artifact or report if permitted.
4. Which systems and exports were required?
5. Where did the process stall or require rework?
6. Which findings were rejected, and why?
7. What happens after a finding is accepted?
8. How often is the work repeated, and who ensures completion?
9. What did the last cycle cost in internal and external hours?
10. Which budget owns the work?
11. What security, procurement, and integration conditions are mandatory?
12. Ask for a paid pilot commitment at a stated price—not a satisfaction score.

Avoid asking whether the idea sounds useful. Past behavior, artifacts, budget,
and a concrete commitment are stronger evidence.

### Usability study — 90 minutes

Give participants a realistic staff roster and access export. Measure time and
errors while they:

- build or import the team;
- resolve unmatched permissions;
- identify the three most important conflicts;
- choose a compensating control;
- assign an owner and evidence request;
- model an absence and a proposed hire;
- approve or reject a remediation;
- export an executive report;
- return later and explain what changed.

Record task completion, wrong turns, assistance, terminology confusion,
confidence, and whether the final conclusion matches an expert answer key.

## 7. Product experiments

### Experiment A — concierge assessment

Run five paid assessments manually with the product supporting—not hiding—the
service. Establish baseline hours and produce a signed finding disposition.
This tests value before expensive integrations.

### Experiment B — CSV actual-access reconciliation

Create a mapping template for user, system, role, permission, location, status,
last-login date, and manager. Measure automatic mapping coverage, unmatched
items, false conflicts, reviewer time, and changes accepted.

### Experiment C — quarterly certification

Run two complete cycles. Measure completion, reminders, delegation, reviewer
disagreement, evidence sufficiency, new/remediated access, and time relative to
the customer's previous method.

### Experiment D — advisor portfolio

Give one advisor three clients, reusable policies, branded reports, and client
isolation. Measure setup reuse, realization, partner review time, and expansion
intent.

### Experiment E — pricing

Test three concrete offers rather than abstract willingness-to-pay questions:

1. fixed-fee assessment with a defined deliverable;
2. assessment plus quarterly monitoring per location;
3. advisor platform plus active-client fee.

Track proposal-to-paid conversion, objections, discounting, implementation
hours, and gross margin. Avoid pricing as a percentage of hypothetical loss.

## 8. Proving value honestly

### Observed value

- hours saved in mapping, review, evidence collection, and reporting;
- external advisory hours avoided or converted to higher-value work;
- direct recoveries and duplicate or unsupported payments stopped;
- aged credits, missed deposits, payroll errors, or access exceptions resolved;
- cycle-time reduction for leavers, certifications, and remediation.

### Control-environment movement

- percentage of critical duties with an owner and backup;
- critical conflicts eliminated, dual-controlled, or formally accepted;
- actual access matching approved access;
- certification and evidence completion;
- median exception age and remediation SLA performance;
- privileged and terminated accounts reviewed on time;
- backup restore-test pass rate.

### Modeled avoided loss

Keep modeled outcomes separate from observed value. Show low/base/high ranges,
source and date every assumption, support overrides with rationale, and never
present an incident-free period as proof of savings.

### Stronger causal evidence

After enough locations exist, use stepped-wedge rollout, matched locations, or
difference-in-differences. Pre-register metrics and exclusions. Track adverse
outcomes as well as positive findings to avoid survivorship bias.

## 9. Product and engineering review plan

### Domain correctness

- golden test cases written and approved by experts;
- precision, recall, and disposition reason for each conflict rule;
- deterministic results independent of array order and import source;
- version migration tests for ontology, templates, controls, and snapshots;
- property tests for score bounds, planner monotonicity, and safe remediation;
- explicit tests for no-person, one-person, outsourced, and owner-override cases.

### Trust boundaries and security

- normalize the entire dual-release policy, not only its containers;
- authorize and tenant-scope every server operation;
- make quotas and writes concurrency-safe;
- use append-only audit events for approvals, imports, restores, and deletion;
- add optimistic concurrency for drafts and accepted baselines;
- document data classification, retention, deletion, backup, and recovery;
- complete threat modeling, dependency scanning, secret scanning, and abuse cases;
- establish SOC 2 and BAA readiness before enterprise or PHI-bearing workflows.

### Reliability and observability

- end-to-end checks for sign-in, authorization isolation, snapshot lifecycle,
  restore, import, comparison, assignment change, and evidence approval;
- structured errors and request correlation without sensitive payload logging;
- server-function latency and failure metrics;
- database migration, backup, and restore drills;
- optional AI timeout, cost ceiling, redaction, and deterministic fallback metrics.

### Accessibility and usability

- keyboard-only navigation and visible focus;
- semantic tabs, tables, dialogs, and status announcements;
- chart and graph alternatives;
- reduced-motion behavior;
- contrast verification and 200% zoom;
- narrow viewport and large-team matrix performance;
- URL-addressable workspace and investigation state.

### Maintainability

- split the largest UI components into domain hooks and focused panels;
- centralize safe browser-storage parsing and version migration;
- add a standard test runner rather than a single growing assertion script;
- document architectural boundaries and prohibited client/server imports;
- measure bundle and render performance in CI.

## 10. Twelve-week validation schedule

### Weeks 1–2 — preparation

- recruit 20 interviewees and the editorial panel;
- define the standard scenario and evidence templates;
- instrument baseline time and outcome measures;
- prepare priced pilot contracts and data-handling terms;
- build CSV access-import prototype and finding-disposition capture.

### Weeks 3–4 — discovery

- complete 12 advisor and 8 group interviews;
- observe four workflows;
- obtain at least five sanitized artifacts;
- issue five priced proposals;
- update the ontology only through recorded editorial decisions.

### Weeks 5–8 — paid design partners

- onboard five paid pilots;
- time every implementation step;
- independently review every critical finding;
- measure mapping coverage, false positives, and remediation acceptance;
- ship only blockers to completing the recurring workflow.

### Weeks 9–10 — second cycle

- repeat certification with the same customers;
- compare completion time, changes, and unresolved exceptions;
- test advisor portfolio reuse and executive reporting;
- collect renewal or expansion decisions.

### Weeks 11–12 — decision

- analyze results against predeclared thresholds;
- calculate gross margin including implementation and support;
- document failed hypotheses and customer segments;
- decide go, narrow/pivot, or stop;
- publish the next six-month roadmap only after the decision.

## 11. Commercial scorecard

| Area | Metric | 12-week gate |
|---|---|---:|
| Demand | Paid design partners | 5 |
| Retention signal | Commit to second cycle | 3 of 5 |
| Channel | Advisor multi-client intent | At least 1 advisor / 3 clients |
| Activation | Credible initial model | Median under 4 advisor hours |
| Accuracy | Critical findings judged valid | At least 70% |
| Actionability | Critical findings accepted for action | At least 50% |
| Data | Permissions automatically mapped | At least 80% |
| Workflow | Certification completed on time | At least 85% |
| Risk | Unresolved critical conflicts at day 90 | Down at least 50% |
| Value | Demonstrable observed value or approved risk closure | At least annual price |
| Economics | Implementation after templates mature | Below 25% of first-year value |

## 12. Go, narrow, or stop

### Continue toward a commercial product when

- at least three paid pilots commit to repeat;
- a buyer and recurring workflow owner are consistently identifiable;
- findings are accurate, actionable, and materially faster to produce;
- one advisor wants multiple clients on the platform;
- customers will maintain actual access and evidence, not only admire the report;
- the delivery model can reach acceptable gross margin without bespoke engineering.

### Narrow or pivot when

- acquisition assessments sell but recurring certification does not;
- advisors use the tool but clients will not self-operate it;
- one workflow—such as AP/payment authority or access certification—creates most
  of the value;
- customers pay for a service-enabled product but not pure SaaS.

In those cases, build the profitable narrow offer rather than forcing a broad
platform narrative.

### Stop treating it as a standalone venture when

- buyers praise the demo but repeatedly refuse paid pilots;
- no one owns the second review cycle;
- finding validity remains below 50% after expert tuning;
- every customer requires unique integrations and policies;
- implementation and support consume most of first-year revenue;
- value depends primarily on hypothetical fraud savings.

The result could still be an excellent internal tool, advisory accelerator, or
open methodology. That is a successful outcome if the evidence does not support
a scalable software company.

Before external use, record retrieval date, document version, exact claim,
excerpt location, reviewer, and supersession date. Initial official targets:

- COSO Internal Control framework and implementation materials;
- GAO Green Book (2025 revision);
- NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0 and small-business resources;
- HHS HIPAA Security Rule risk-analysis guidance;
- FBI business-email-compromise guidance;
- ADA and state dental-association practice-management guidance;
- official product, security, integration, and help-center materials for each
  benchmarked vendor;
- customer contracts, audit reports, access exports, and workflow artifacts
  obtained with permission.

Automated web access was unavailable during preparation of this plan, so it
does not assert newly verified competitor features, prices, or market sizes.
Those are explicit research tasks—not assumptions disguised as findings.
